The Weekend Gap Protection Guide: Managing Open Risk Before the Friday Close

7 min read Updated August 22, 2026 Fewpips Team

Short answer: Weekend gap risk is the chance that a market reopens Monday at a price far from where it closed Friday, because news during the closed period never gets reflected until trading resumes. That gap can blow past a stop loss and disqualify an otherwise good trade. The fix is not to panic on Friday afternoon. It is to run the same pre-weekend risk check every single week, so the decision is already made before the deadline shows up.

This is one of the first rules serious traders check before they ever fund a challenge, because it decides whether a strategy can actually be traded the way it was built, or whether it has to be squeezed into a five-day box. We built this guide around exactly that question.

Why Weekend Gap Risk Is a Bigger Deal Than Most Traders Admit

Most traders think about risk in terms of the trade itself. Entry, stop, target, done. Weekend gap risk sits outside that whole framework, and that is exactly why it catches people off guard.

Here is the mechanic. Forex and CFD markets do not run 24/7. They run 24/5. Once the New York session winds down Friday evening, liquidity dries up and most venues stay closed until Sydney opens Sunday night. The world does not pause during that stretch. Central banks make surprise statements. Elections happen. Headlines break. None of it shows up on your chart until the market reopens.

When it reopens, price does not ease back in from wherever it left off. It can open sharply higher or lower than Friday's close, skipping straight over every level in between. That jump is the gap. If you were holding through it, your stop loss does not protect you the way it normally would, because stops fill at the next available price once trading resumes, not at the exact level you set. In a violent gap, you can exit well beyond where you planned.

The Swiss National Bank's 2015 decision to drop the franc's currency peg is still the go-to example in trading education. It was not technically a weekend gap, but it shows the same principle in fast motion. One unexpected announcement moved EUR/CHF thousands of pips in minutes, and traders holding positions had no real way to react. Weekend gaps run on that same logic, just with two extra days of silence built in before the move actually hits your account.

Why So Many Prop Firms Force a Friday Close

If gap risk is a market-wide problem, why do so many funded programs build a hard rule around it? The honest answer is liability management. A firm is not just watching your single position. It is watching every funded trader's position gapping against them at the same time during one bad weekend. Forcing everyone to flatten by Friday afternoon is the simplest way for a firm to reset its own drawdown exposure to zero every week, no matter what happens between sessions.

For the trader, that rule has a real cost. If your edge depends on multi-day price action, a forced Friday close cuts the legs out from under it before the setup has time to work. You end up closing winners early because a clock told you to, not because your analysis did, and you usually do it during the most illiquid, spread-widened hour of the week. That is a quiet tax on your edge that never shows up as a single obvious loss, just a slow bleed of missed profit and bad fills.

What a Forced Friday Close Actually Costs You

Traders tend to underestimate how expensive a rushed close really is. It is not just missing a bit of extra profit if the trade kept running. Run the math on a simple example.

Say you are holding a $50,000 account position with an open profit of $600 late Friday afternoon, and your target is another 15 pips away. Spreads on your pair widen from 1 pip to 4 pips as liquidity pulls back into the close. On a standard lot, that difference alone can cost $30 to $40 just to exit, on top of whatever profit you leave on the table by closing before your target hits. Multiply that across a month of Fridays and it adds up to real, avoidable drag on your account.

Three costs stack up every time this happens:

  • Spread and slippage. Liquidity thins right as a wave of other traders flatten positions for the same rule-based reasons you are, so you pay more to exit at the worst possible moment for execution quality.
  • Premature exits on winners. A position that is up but has not hit target yet still has to close, locking in less profit than your plan called for, purely because of the calendar.
  • Deadline-driven decisions. Rushed decisions are rarely good ones. Traders who normally manage risk calmly start overriding their own plans once a clock, not their analysis, is forcing the exit.

None of this means weekend rules are unreasonable. They exist for real structural reasons. It means you need a repeatable process for managing them instead of scrambling every Friday afternoon.

A Practical Friday Risk Audit

Whether your program requires a full flat close or lets you decide for yourself, running the same checklist every Friday takes the emotion out of it. Treat it as a routine, not a one-off decision you make under pressure.

  • Check the calendar first, not the chart. Look at what is scheduled for Sunday evening and the week ahead: central bank meetings, elections, major data releases. A known catalyst changes your weekend risk profile more than anything else on your screen. It is also why Fewpips restricts new trades during high-impact news windows in the first place. The rule is not there to slow you down. It is there because that is exactly when spreads blow out and gaps get violent.
  • Scale down before you shut down. If you are allowed to hold but you are uneasy about size, trimming a position to a smaller lot is usually smarter than closing it outright or leaving it at full risk.
  • Move your stop to protect the trade, not just cap the loss. Tightening a stop toward breakeven or into profit before the weekend turns an open risk into a much smaller decision.
  • Take partial profits on anything already working. Banking a portion of a winning trade takes pressure off whatever is left open, whichever way the market gaps.
  • Know your firm's actual rule, in writing, not from memory. "I think we're allowed to hold" is not a risk plan. Read the current terms before every Friday, because rules change between programs and sometimes between account types at the same firm.

When It Actually Makes Sense To Hold Through The Weekend

Not every open position deserves a weekend pass, even when your program allows it. A good rule of thumb: if the trade's thesis is still intact, the size is something you would be comfortable holding through a surprise headline, and no major scheduled catalyst specifically threatens the instrument you are in, holding can be the more disciplined choice, not the riskier one. Closing a perfectly good trade purely out of nervousness is not risk management either. It is just a different kind of undisciplined trading.

Frequently Asked Questions

What Is Weekend Gap Risk in Trading?

It is the possibility that a market reopens at a meaningfully different price than where it closed, because news during the closed period is not reflected until trading resumes. Stop losses can fill far from their set level as a result.

Why Do Prop Firms Restrict Holding Trades Over the Weekend?

Mostly to control their own aggregate drawdown exposure across all funded accounts at once, since a single volatile weekend event could otherwise trigger large, correlated losses across the firm's book.

Do All Prop Firms Require a Friday Close?

No. Requirements vary by firm and sometimes by account type within the same firm. Fewpips allows weekend holding on every CFD challenge type, from Instant accounts through the 3-Step program, while Future Based CFD accounts close before the weekend.

How Can I Protect an Open Position Before the Weekend If I Have to Close It?

Scale out gradually through the week instead of all at once on Friday, bank partial profits on anything already in your favor, and avoid opening new trades late Friday that you would be forced to exit in thin, wide-spread conditions.
